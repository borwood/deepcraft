# Things that will happen in this game

A one-pager of concrete, informative examples — **the mental model, not the
mechanisms.** Created 2026-07-20 at the user's instruction: *"probably we
need one-pager to append one-line 'things that will happen in this game'
whenever a genuinely informative new example pops up, so new sessions can
start with the right mental model."*

**Rules for this file.** One line per example. Append only. Add a line the
moment a genuinely *informative* example surfaces in conversation — one
that would change how a reader imagines the game, not merely restate a
feature. Read this file at the start of a session, before the design docs;
it is the fastest way to load what this game actually is. Not a promise
list and not a roadmap — some of these are far off, and that is fine.

---

- You dig 200 m down, hit a wall that erosion refused to cut, and the strata
  *above* it tell you how long it has been there.
- You mine a coal seam that exists because a swamp sat at base level, quietly,
  for millions of years — and the rock above it records the flood that ended it.
- You breach the floor of a lake into a cave below, and the lake drains into
  it. Permanently. The basin is dry from then on.
- You dig a channel a kilometre from a river and water runs to the far end,
  because it is the same body of water, not a copy of it.
- You find a cave that used to be underwater: dissolved smooth below the old
  water table, dry now, growing flowstone from the drips still coming through.
- You read a cliff face and know the sea came and went, because the layers say
  so and nobody wrote them.
- The sun low on the horizon shines into a cave mouth, through a window, under
  an overhang — and the simulation knows it does, not just the shader.
- Your torch's reach depends on what you made it out of. A better material
  lights more of the room.
- The dark is dangerous, and light is equipment you carry, maintain and run
  out of.
- Caves have their own ecology, because ecology is a diagnosis of conditions
  and "no light, stable temperature, wet, fed from outside" is a set of
  conditions like any other.
- A creature sees a band of light you cannot, and behaves accordingly.
- A modder adds a second moon on a different axis, and the world's nights,
  tides and night-blooming things all change without the modder touching any
  of them.
- Ancient aliens built impenetrable outposts in geological prehistory, and the
  whole downstream simulation takes it seriously — burial, exhumation, the lot.
- The sun's arc across your sky depends on where you are standing, so poles
  and tropics are somewhere you travel to, not a setting anyone chose.
- You pan gold flakes from a gravel bar. The vein they eroded out of is
  somewhere upstream — so you read the river, and walk upstream.
- A coal seam dead-ends at a fault. The white ash stripe forty meters up —
  the same afternoon, four million years ago, everywhere it appears — is
  offset by the same throw. Follow it, find your seam again.
- A land can be a desert in every way that matters to its rocks and its life,
  yet look green from a hill — because "arid" is a verdict about water
  balance, not a colour, and the two only sometimes agree.
- A pond laps quietly; an ocean coast is carved by storms — because wave
  energy is a fact about the body of water (its reach, depth, and the wind
  across it over millennia), not a setting someone chose.
- You undercut a grassy slope and the soil above slumps into your hole —
  because what was holding it up was a root lattice with soil packed in its
  pores, and you cut the lattice.
- The edge of a dune is a hand's depth of sand, not a stair-step of cubes:
  the world reports the fraction it actually has.
- You dig through loose topsoil into subsoil that resists the shovel, and the
  difference is real — the same material, packed by everything that has sat
  on top of it since.
- Two fields in the same climate, one a sink and one a scoured shoulder: you
  shovel ten metres of fill in the first and hit bedrock immediately in the
  second, because soil depth is a fact about erosion history, not rainfall.
- The place where the wind took the most is not the place where the least
  remains — the great deflation basins are deep because they had the most to
  lose, and they are still losing it.
- A block at a bed contact is *both* rocks, in the proportion the boundary
  actually cuts it — and the block beside it holds a different proportion,
  because the world quantized honestly instead of picking a winner.
- A hand's width of volcanic ash, laid down in an afternoon four million years
  ago, is still findable — not as its own layer, which is thinner than a
  block, but as a trace mixed through the blocks that swallowed it.
- The ground is the colour of what the ground is made of. There is no skin
  painted over it, so a dune field reads as its own sand and the bare shoulder
  beside it reads as the granite it was scoured down to — and the horizon
  behind them agrees, because it is the same answer sampled further away.
- You dig up through a confining bed and the shaft fills — you have punched
  into an unconfined aquifer, and the water table is now a thing you can see
  the top of.
- You follow a cave down and it ends in a flooded gorge: the passage kept
  going, the water table did not.
- The gravel bar you are standing on holds water in the spaces between its
  grains; the granite behind it holds water in its cracks. Both are aquifers
  and they behave nothing alike.
- Rain falls on a slope and some of it runs off, some soaks in, and some is
  simply gone by afternoon — and which one dominates is a fact about what the
  slope is made of.
- A spring is not placed anywhere. It is where the water table meets the
  hillside, so draining the hill upstream moves it or kills it.
- There is a place where the ground is warm and the water comes out hot,
  because the seam you followed goes down to where the rock is hot.
- Somewhere in the world there is a canyon you could spend a day walking into,
  a lake big enough to have weather, and an inland sea with no outlet that is
  saltier every century.
- You cut a face through a floodplain and there are black specks in the mud —
  not a band, just specks, and only in the layers where a fire came through.
  You cannot mine them. They tell you the forest here burned.
- The coal seam you are standing on is thin, and it is coal because there is a
  hundred metres of rock on top of it. The nine-metre peat bed a valley over is
  still peat, because nothing ever buried it.
- You dig a pit-mine for a week. Walking home from five kilometres out, the
  scar is on the skyline, because the far field is a summary of the world and
  not a picture of it.
- You stand at the lip of a chasm and look down, and the deep dark is coarse
  but real geology — the actual strata, blurred by distance — not fog.
- Where one soil gives way to another the ground does not switch at a line — it
  interfingers, a scatter of the old material thinning into the new over the
  span of a hillside, because a 460 m cell that is 55/45 two soils skins 55/45
  of its columns, not all of them the winner.
